# MyUni AI Playbook

**Source note:** built from established, general creator-content principles
(hook theory, retention pacing, thumbnail psychology) plus what's already
proven inside this project (the 9.8/10-clickability aerial-dunk composition,
the quality-gate results across 6 real builds). **Not** derived from any
specific reference video — none was attached when this was requested. If
you have a specific video in mind, send the link/file and this gets a real
update, not a guess.

This is the standard every NBA 2K27 video now runs through — encoded into
`scriptsAgent.js` and `brandingAgent.js`'s prompts, not just documented here.

---

## Brand voice (the one rule that overrides everything else)

**Confident. Clear. High-energy. Authentic.**

Explicitly **not** a generic "urban" aesthetic chased because the genre is
basketball/streetball — that's a costume, not a voice, and it reads as
generic rather than premium. The voice comes from *substance* (real
attribute numbers, real badge reasoning, real tradeoffs stated plainly) and
*delivery* (short sentences, direct address, zero hedging), not from slang
or a borrowed persona. A script should sound like a confident creator who
knows this build cold — not like it's performing an identity.

- **Confident**: state claims plainly. "This is the versatile day-one
  build" — not "this might be a pretty solid build maybe."
- **Clear**: every claim traces to a real number/badge in the spec. No
  vague hype ("this build is insane") without the stat backing it up.
- **High-energy**: short sentences, active verbs, forward momentum — energy
  from pacing and specificity, not from forced slang.
- **Authentic**: talk to the viewer like a peer who already plays the game,
  not down to them, not at them with marketing copy.

## Hook framework

The first line has one job: create an information gap the viewer needs
closed. Three proven patterns, in order of strength for this content type:

1. **Contradiction/tension**: "Everybody's rushing to build a shooter, and
   then they get switch-hunted all game." (sets up why the build is the
   answer to a real frustration)
2. **Direct capability claim + a number**: "A 6'6 point guard with 90
   playmaking AND Hall of Fame Downhill?" (specificity is the hook, not
   adjectives)
3. **Second-person stakes**: "What if you didn't have to choose between a
   shooter and a big?" (names the tradeoff the viewer is actually weighing)

Never open with the game name, a generic greeting, or "in this video."

## Structure (long-form)

1. **Hook** (0:00-0:10) — one of the three patterns above.
2. **Frame** — position/height/weight/wingspan, and *why* that frame
   matters mechanically (not just stating the numbers).
3. **Attributes, ranked by what carries the build** — lead with the
   highest/defining number, explain what it *does*, not just what it *is*.
4. **Badges** — each one tied back to a specific attribute/action, never
   listed without a reason.
5. **Takeovers** — how they combine with what's already been established.
6. **The loop** — one sentence that ties attributes + badges + takeovers
   into a repeatable in-game pattern. This is the retention anchor: it's
   the thing a viewer remembers after the video ends.
7. **Who it's for** — names the specific frustration this build solves.
8. **CTA** — invite replication, not just subscription ("go build it" beats
   "smash that like button").

## Retention beats

A viewer decides to keep watching roughly every 30-45 seconds. Each section
above should open with a *new* concrete fact (a number, a badge name, a
specific interaction) — never restate the previous section's point in
different words. If a section has no new number or name in its first
sentence, it's a drop-off risk.

## Pacing

Short sentences carry energy; long sentences are for the one moment per
video where you slow down for a genuinely important tradeoff (the
defense/rebounding sacrifice, e.g.). Vary sentence length on purpose — never
more than 2 consecutive sentences of the same rhythm.

## Thumbnail psychology

- **One dominant subject, ~40-60% of frame** — never a small subject lost
  in the lower third.
- **Information gap, not information dump** — a thumbnail should make
  someone need to click to resolve a question ("what build is THAT"), not
  answer every question before the click.
- **Action beats posture** — a shot mid-dunk outperforms a static
  crossed-arms portrait for click-through; save the calm portrait for the
  video body, not the thumbnail.
- **High contrast, saturated color grade** — must read at ~120px wide in a
  sidebar; if the hero element doesn't read at that size, the composition
  is wrong, not the resolution.
- **No baked text by default** — headline as a typographic overlay on top
  of a clean render reads more legibly and costs nothing to iterate on,
  versus re-rendering the whole image to fix a typo.

## Emotional arc

Confidence (the hook) → tension (the tradeoff/frustration named) →
resolution (why this build solves it) → payoff (the highlight moment,
visually and narratively) → invitation (go build it yourself). Every
generated script and shot list should trace this arc, not just list facts
in spec order.

## Applying this to a second (or Nth) build

This playbook is generic to the *format*, not to any one build — the hook
pattern, structure, retention-beat discipline, and thumbnail psychology
apply identically whether the build is a 6'2 guard or a 7'2 center. What
changes per build is only the substance (the actual numbers/badges/
tradeoffs), never the delivery style. That consistency — same voice, same
structural discipline, different specifics — is what makes builds read as
one channel instead of independently-styled one-offs.
